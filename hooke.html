Hooke's Law

Every spring has its own stiffness, expressed by its spring constant k. In this lab you are given a mystery spring — you don't know its k. Hang different weights from it, measure how far it stretches, and work out k for yourself. The spring is a little non-linear, so be careful: real springs only follow Hooke's law approximately.

1 Theory

A short reminder of what Hooke's law says and how to get the spring constant from measurements.

The law

For a spring pulled within its elastic limit, the extension is proportional to the force applied:

F = k · e Hooke's law: F is the force (N), k the spring constant (N/m), e the extension (m)

Rearranged, the spring constant is the force per unit extension:

k = F / e Units: newtons per metre (N/m). A stiff spring has a large k.

The force is the weight of the hanging masses: F = W = m·g, so the extension grows in proportion to the mass. If you plot F on the y-axis against extension e on the x-axis, Hooke's law predicts a straight line through the origin, and the gradient is k.

Measuring extension correctly

  • Extension = new length − original length: e = L − L₀. Always subtract the original length, because the spring already has some length with no load. This is the "change in length" you measure in this lab.
  • Hang the weights gently and wait for the spring to come to rest before reading the pointer.
  • Take every reading against the same pointer, and read to the smallest division (here, millimetres).
  • Repeat and average if you can.
Real springs aren't perfectly linear. Hooke's law is an idealisation. In practice the force is not exactly proportional to the extension: the spring gets a little stiffer (or softer) as it stretches. On a force–extension graph this shows up as a line that curves slightly. The ratio F/e therefore drifts a little as you add weight — you should average your values (or find the best-fit gradient) rather than trust a single point.

How this lab works

You have one spring of unknown, randomly chosen stiffness (its true k lies somewhere between 5 and 50 N/m) and a slightly non-linear force–extension response. You also have a small selection of weights to choose from. There is a ruler fixed beside the spring. The New spring button swaps in a fresh spring with a different stiffness at any time. The plan:

  1. Read the spring's original length with no weights on it.
  2. Hang one weight, let it settle, and read the new length — take e = L − L₀.
  3. Repeat for each weight, recording force and extension.
  4. Tabulate F vs e and find the gradient = k. Compare values to see the non-linearity.

You can choose whichever weights you like and re-run the experiment as often as you like — but the spring itself is the same every time, so your estimate of k should agree across different loads (to within the non-linearity).

Questions to think about

  • Does the value of k = F/e stay constant as you add more weight, or does it drift? What does that tell you about how linear this spring is?
  • Why do we measure extension from the original length rather than just the length?
  • What would the force–extension graph look like for a stiffer spring (larger k)?
  • The spring constant is a property of the spring, not the load. Does your data support that?
